HoldLight

Making Indoor Climbing More Accessible for Blind and Low-Vision Climbers through Layered Route Preview and Real-Time Next-Hold Guidance

Chenyu Du, Junyan He, Jiafei Sun, Yue Li

Read paper ↗ Watch demo Source code

RoleFirst author · System development and evaluation

Study12 blind and low-vision climbers · Counterbalanced mixed methods

SupervisorYue Li

The problem

Blind and low-vision climbers need access to route information both before they begin climbing and while moving between holds. HoldLight investigates how layered previews and timely guidance can support route understanding and perceived autonomy.

What I contributed

I conceived and led development of the system, translating formative questionnaires and interviews into layered route previews and action-timed audio cues. I implemented rule-based route planning, server-assisted wall alignment, and pose tracking for in-climb guidance.

The interaction includes user-controlled clarification and fallback to human assistance when the system is uncertain.

How the interaction works

  1. Capture the wall. Prepare visual information for identifying holds and planning a route.
  2. Preview in layers. Explore route information before beginning the climb.
  3. Receive timely guidance. Use pose tracking and audio cues to support the next move.
  4. Clarify or seek assistance. Retain human support when information is uncertain.

Evaluation and findings

I designed and conducted a counterbalanced mixed-method study with 12 blind and low-vision climbers. The study compared HoldLight with controlled on-demand human support and examined route understanding, uncertainty, and perceived autonomy.

The findings included higher route-understanding scores and fewer requests for routine human route-information support. These findings concern information access; human safety support remained available throughout the climbing workflow.

Design process and evidence

The paper provides the complete research report. The process portfolio contains design exploration, prototypes, and implementation materials.

RoboBlockly Studio

Conversational Block Programming with Embodied Robot Feedback for Computational Thinking

Leyi Li*, Chenyu Du*, Jiafei Sun, Erick Purwanto, Qing Zhang
* Equal contribution

Read paper ↗ DOI

RoleCo-first author · Technical design and implementation; study contributions

Study5 instructor interviews · 32 novice high-school students

SupervisorErick Purwanto

The problem

Learning to program involves connecting abstract code to observable behavior. RoboBlockly Studio brings block programming, conversational AI, and a physical robot into one learning workflow.

What I contributed

I co-led technical design and implementation, including Blockly-to-Python conversion, task-grounded conversational AI feedback, WebSocket deployment, and execution on a Jetson Orin Nano / ROS 2 robot.

I translated findings from interviews with five computer science instructors into the learning workflow and contributed to study design and execution.

A loop of authoring and observation

  1. Author. Express a task through block-based programs.
  2. Ask. Receive conversational feedback grounded in the task and the learner’s program.
  3. Run and observe. Connect program execution to the physical robot’s behavior.
  4. Revise. Use observations to debug the program and ask more targeted questions.

Evaluation and findings

The work included a counterbalanced within-subject study with 32 novice high-school students. I analyzed task-performance and workload data alongside interview accounts.

The AI+Robot condition revealed a shift toward execution-based error checking and more targeted AI queries. The full paper documents the conditions, measures, and analysis.
